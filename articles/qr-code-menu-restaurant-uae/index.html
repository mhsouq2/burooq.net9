QR Code Menu for Restaurants in the UAE: How to Set One Up the Right Way

A QR menu lets guests scan a code on the table and see your dishes, photos and prices on their own phone. Done well, it saves reprinting and speeds up ordering. Done badly, it frustrates guests. Here is what works in UAE restaurants and cafés.

Written by Alburooq's software team in Abu Dhabi

Short answer

A good QR code menu for a UAE restaurant is a mobile web page, not an app and not a blurry PDF. It opens in one scan, loads fast on weak signal, lets guests switch between Arabic and English, shows prices that already include VAT, and can be edited by the owner in seconds when a price changes or a dish sells out.

Keep a few printed menus for guests who prefer paper, put a clear, scratch-resistant QR card on every table, and test it on an old phone before opening day.

How a QR menu works

Behind every QR code is a web address. When a guest points their phone camera at the code, the phone offers to open that address in the browser. What they see next depends entirely on what you put at that address:

  • A PDF file. Cheapest option. Guests pinch and zoom to read a page designed for A4 paper. Every price change means editing and re-uploading the file.
  • A menu web page. Dishes grouped into sections (breakfast, mains, drinks), each with a photo, short description and price. Text fits the phone screen and the owner edits it from a dashboard.
  • An ordering system. The menu plus a cart, so guests send the order to the kitchen or counter. This needs a restaurant POS behind it and staff trained to handle the orders.

Most cafés and small restaurants in Abu Dhabi get the best value from the second option: a proper menu web page. Ordering from the table can come later once the team is comfortable.

Why UAE restaurants are moving to QR menus

Prices change, printing costs add up

Supplier prices for meat, vegetables and coffee move during the year. With paper menus, every change means a new print run or stickers over old prices, which guests notice. A digital menu is updated from the owner's phone and every table sees the new price straight away.

Two languages, many nationalities

A typical café in Khalifa City or Mussafah serves Emirati families, Arab expats, South Asian workers and Western residents in the same afternoon. Fitting full Arabic and English on one printed page makes the text tiny. On a phone, guests tap one button to switch language and the text stays readable.

Heat, dust and hygiene

Laminated menus on outdoor terraces fade in the sun and collect dust and grease. Guests also handle them all day. A small QR stand or table sticker is easier to wipe clean, and replacing it costs very little.

Fewer "Is this available?" questions

When a dish runs out, staff hide it from the menu in a few seconds. Guests stop ordering what the kitchen cannot make, and waiters spend less time apologising.

What your QR menu must include in the UAE

1. Prices that include VAT

The Federal Tax Authority has told businesses registered for VAT that the prices they display must include tax, so the customer sees the real amount they will pay. Gulf News reported the FTA's warning that showing prices without tax is treated as a violation, with a fine per tax type. Your digital menu should follow the same rule as a printed one: the number next to each dish is the final price. If you add a service charge, say so clearly on the menu.

2. Arabic and English

Write dish names and descriptions in both languages, and have an Arabic speaker check the Arabic. Machine translations of dish names often read oddly or mean something else. The Arabic view should read right to left with proper alignment, not just English layout with Arabic words.

3. Clear sections and photos

Keep sections short and in the order people eat: starters, mains, sides, desserts, drinks. Use real photos of your own dishes taken in good light. Stock photos set expectations the kitchen cannot meet.

4. Allergen and dietary notes

Add simple icons or words for common allergens such as nuts, dairy, gluten and seafood, and mark vegetarian dishes. Guests with allergies will still ask staff, but the menu should never hide the information.

5. Healthy-dish labels for Abu Dhabi hotel outlets

If your restaurant operates under a tourism licence from the Department of Culture and Tourism – Abu Dhabi, DCT Circular No. 7 of 2026 requires at least one healthy option in each adult menu category, at least half of children's menu items to be healthy options, and qualifying dishes to be marked with the approved SEHHI logo following ADPHC guidance. Full enforcement started on 1 October 2026, and buffets and tasting menus are among the listed exclusions. The National covered the rollout. The circular talks about "menus" in general, so if you use a QR menu, plan to show the same label on the digital version and confirm the details with ADPHC.

Step-by-step: setting up a QR menu

  1. Collect your current menu. Dish names in Arabic and English, descriptions, VAT-inclusive prices, and allergen notes. A spreadsheet is fine.
  2. Take photos. Daylight near a window, plain background, same angle for every dish. A recent phone camera is enough.
  3. Choose the platform. Make sure you can edit prices yourself, switch languages, hide items, and add your logo and colours.
  4. Get the QR codes. One code can serve every table. If you plan table ordering later, ask for one code per table so orders show the table number.
  5. Print durable table cards. Matte finish reduces glare under café lights. For outdoor seating choose UV-resistant stickers or acrylic stands so the code does not fade in summer.
  6. Add a short instruction. "Scan for menu / امسح لعرض المنيو" next to the code, plus your WiFi name if you offer guest WiFi.
  7. Test on real phones. Try an older Android and an iPhone, on mobile data and on your WiFi. The menu should open in a few seconds.
  8. Train staff. Show waiters how to hide a sold-out item and how to help a guest who cannot scan.

Common mistakes we see

  • The QR code points to a free trial that expires. Months later the code opens an error page. Use a service where you control the link and renewal.
  • Weak signal inside the restaurant. Basements, malls and thick villa walls can block mobile data. Guest WiFi or a WiFi booster fixes this; otherwise guests stare at a loading screen.
  • Codes printed too small or on glossy paper. Aim for at least 3 cm across and keep a white border around the code.
  • Arabic as an afterthought. Half-translated menus look careless to Arabic-speaking guests.
  • No paper backup. Some guests prefer paper. Keep a few printed menus behind the counter.
