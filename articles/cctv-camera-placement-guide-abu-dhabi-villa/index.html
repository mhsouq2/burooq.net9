Most villa CCTV systems in Abu Dhabi aren't let down by bad cameras — they're let down by bad placement. We've walked into more than one post-incident review where every camera on the property was working perfectly, recording in full HD, and still somehow missed the one angle that mattered. The wall was too high, the lens was pointed at the sun, or the "coverage" existed on paper but not in the actual footage.

Camera placement is a design problem, not an installation afterthought. Get it right and eight cameras cover a property properly. Get it wrong and sixteen cameras still leave gaps. This guide walks through where blind spots actually form on a typical Abu Dhabi villa and how to place cameras so the system covers what you actually need it to.

Why placement matters more than camera count

It's tempting to solve coverage anxiety by adding more cameras. But two cameras aimed correctly at a gate and driveway will out-perform six cameras scattered without a plan. Camera count affects your NVR storage requirements and cabling cost; placement affects whether the system actually does its job. Before adding a camera, the right question is always "what specific event does this camera need to capture, and from where?" — not "where's a convenient spot to mount something."

Start with the perimeter: gates, walls, and the driveway

The single most valuable camera on most villa systems is the one covering the main vehicle gate. This is where the majority of incidents — attempted break-ins, disputes with delivery or service staff, vehicle damage — actually happen, and it's the one angle owners most often check first after something goes wrong.

  • Mount height: 2.7–3.2 meters is the sweet spot for most villa gates. Lower, and the camera is an easy target for tampering or a ladder. Higher, and you lose usable facial detail — a camera mounted 5 meters up captures a great overview shot but a nearly useless face for identification.
  • Angle: Point the camera down the driveway toward the gate, not straight down at it. A steep downward angle gives you the top of someone's head, which is worthless for identification. A camera angled to capture someone's face as they approach or as they operate the gate intercom is worth far more.
  • Pair it with a second camera facing outward from just inside the gate, covering the street and any vehicles that pull up outside. This is the angle that actually identifies a vehicle's plate or a person loitering before an incident, rather than only capturing them once they're already on the property.

Side and rear boundary walls are the second priority. In villa compounds across Khalifa City, Al Reef, and Al Raha, we regularly find rear walls with zero coverage simply because "nobody comes in from the back" — until someone does, usually because it's the side nobody's watching.

The front door and majlis entrance

The main entrance deserves its own dedicated camera, separate from the gate camera, mounted under the eave or portico directly above the door — high enough to avoid easy reach, angled to capture anyone standing at the door rather than just the top of their head. This is the camera that matters most for package deliveries, visitor disputes, and confirming who actually rang the bell versus who claims they did.

If your villa has a separate majlis entrance used by guests, it earns its own camera too. Majlis areas often see more foot traffic from people outside the immediate family, and owners frequently want a record of who came and went — particularly during gatherings.

Garden, pool, and rear terrace

These areas are lower priority from a security standpoint but high priority from a household-safety standpoint — most owners we install for want garden and pool coverage primarily to keep an eye on children and staff, not to catch intruders. A single wide-angle camera mounted under a rear eave, positioned to capture the pool edge and the main garden seating area, usually covers this need without over-specifying additional units.

One placement mistake we see often: mounting a garden camera facing directly into the afternoon sun. Abu Dhabi's low western sun in late afternoon will wash out a poorly positioned camera for hours a day. Orient garden and pool cameras north or east-facing where possible, or budget for a camera with strong wide dynamic range (WDR) if a west-facing angle is unavoidable.

Garage, side passage, and utility areas

Side passages between the villa and the boundary wall are a classic blind spot — narrow, often unlit, and rarely walked past by the household, which makes them exactly where an intruder would rather be than in front of a well-lit, camera-covered gate. A single camera at each end of a side passage, angled down its length, closes this gap cheaply.

Garages and external storage/utility rooms benefit from indoor cameras where valuable equipment, tools, or vehicles are kept — this is also useful for confirming garage door operation and catching any issue with automatic gate motors or garage door sensors before it becomes a bigger repair.

NVR and recorder placement (the part owners forget)

Camera placement gets attention; NVR/DVR placement usually doesn't, and it should. The recorder needs to sit somewhere with reasonable ventilation, away from direct sun and away from a garage or storage room that regularly hits extreme temperatures in summer — heat is one of the leading causes of early hard drive failure in villa systems. Ideally, mount it in a store room or a dedicated utility space with airflow, not sealed inside a hot cabinet.

How many cameras does this actually add up to?

For a typical Abu Dhabi villa, a properly placed system usually looks like this:

  • 1 camera on the main gate (angled toward approaching visitors)
  • 1 camera facing outward from just inside the gate (street/vehicle view)
  • 1 camera on the front door/entrance
  • 1–2 cameras on side passages
  • 1 camera on the rear boundary wall
  • 1 camera covering garden/pool
  • Optional: majlis entrance, garage interior, driveway-side coverage for larger plots

That's 6–8 cameras for most standard villa plots — fewer than owners often assume they need, as long as each one is placed to do a specific job rather than scattered for general reassurance. Common placement mistakes we fix on service calls

  • Cameras aimed at reflective surfaces — glass doors, white walls, or pool water at certain times of day cause glare that a camera never fully recovers from without repositioning.
  • No overlap between adjacent cameras — a true blind spot exists wherever two cameras' fields of view don't touch. A short walk around the property with the live view open on a phone is the easiest way to catch this before installation is finalized.
  • Cameras mounted too high "for safety" — protecting a camera from tampering is a fair goal, but past a certain height the footage stops being useful for actually identifying anyone.
  • Ignoring the sun's seasonal path — a camera that's perfectly exposed in December can be blown out by direct sun for two hours a day by June. A good installer accounts for this before mounting, not after a complaint.

Getting it right the first time

A proper site survey — walking the property, discussing what each area is actually meant to protect against, and testing camera angles before final mounting — is what separates a system that looks complete from one that actually covers your villa. We do this as a standard part of every installation across Abu Dhabi, and we're also happy to review and reposition an existing system that isn't performing the way it should.
